The Critical Quest for Rare Earths

Rare Earth Elements (REEs) are a group of 17 metallic elements essential for modern technologies, including smartphones, electric vehicles, and wind turbines. As global demand surges, the need for efficient and cost-effective exploration methods has become paramount. Traditional exploration often relies on extensive, costly, and time-consuming geophysical surveys and fieldwork. However, recent advancements in remote sensing offer a revolutionary alternative.

This whitepaper explores how our AI-powered hyperspectral platform is bridging the technology gap, enabling the direct detection of REE signatures from the sky with unprecedented accuracy and efficiency.

Seeing the Unseen: Hyperspectral Bands for REE

A hyperspectral sensor captures data across hundreds of narrow spectral bands. For REE exploration, the most critical regions are within the Visible and Near-Infrared (VNIR) and Short-Wave Infrared (SWIR) parts of the spectrum, typically from 400 nm to 2500 nm.

Rare earth elements, particularly Neodymium (Nd), exhibit diagnostic absorption features—sharp dips in the reflectance curve—at very specific wavelengths. As shown in the graph below, key features for Nd in the mineral Bastnaesite appear around 579 nm, 741 nm, 796-801 nm, and 864-889 nm. By measuring the precise depth and shape of these absorption features across a landscape, we can map the concentration of the target mineral.

The AI Powerhouse: Our Spatial-Spectral Transformer

A single hyperspectral survey generates terabytes of complex, multi-dimensional data. To unlock the insights hidden within, we use a proprietary AI model: the Spatial-Spectral Transformer. Unlike traditional methods that analyze spatial (the "where") and spectral (the "what") information separately, our transformer architecture processes them jointly, leading to a much deeper contextual understanding.

Overcoming Exploration Challenges

While incredibly powerful, hyperspectral imaging is not without its challenges. Understanding these factors is key to a successful mission.

Vegetation cover can interfere with REE detection. The chlorophyll in plants has strong absorption features that can mask the subtler signals from minerals below, especially the REE feature at ~580 nm. Therefore, this technology is most effective in arid to semi-arid regions with sparse vegetation cover.

The host mineralogy and background constituents play a significant role. High concentrations of iron oxides can dampen or suppress REE spectral features. Conversely, bright backgrounds like the calcic carbonatite at Mountain Pass can enhance the detectability of REEs.

The ability to detect REEs depends on multiple factors including Nd concentration, mineral grain size, and sensor quality. While the exact detection limit for a drone system varies, the underlying spectroscopic method is highly sensitive, with lab studies detecting Nd concentrations from 1000 to less than 200 ppm.
